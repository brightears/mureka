# Replace random jitter with musical structure

The first Christmas pilot renders sound amateurish for reasons you can see in the generator code, and none of them needs an original recording to fix. The piano tracks run at one fixed tempo with white-noise timing jitter and the same swell every four bars, so they have no rubato, no phrase endings and no final ritardando. The string tracks hold block chords for the full chord length with no dynamics controller movement and no correction for legato latency. Every acoustic arrangement sits at 60-68 BPM on mostly hymn harmony, and the texture changes too little between passes. Performance research gives concrete replacement targets. Skilled pianists sound the melody **about 20-30 ms ahead** of the accompaniment and strike it **roughly 1.3-2 times harder**. Phrases shape tempo and loudness together. Final ritardandi follow a formula that fits measured performances with **mean r² = 0.98**. Listeners prefer long-range-correlated timing deviations to the random ones a DAW "humanize" button produces. Arranging craft is the bigger lever, though: open left-hand spacing, a harmony voice under the melody, fills in every held note, a reharmonized second pass, an intro, one gentle modulation and a tag ending. For strings, the main tell is a static sustain. Drawn dynamic curves per section, monophonic legato lines and per-articulation negative delay fix most of it. No MCP server, Claude skill or AI model composes at this level. The tools that help are Pianist Transformer (Apache-2.0) as an A/B for piano performance, an Ableton MCP for loading devices, Spotify's Pedalboard for headless rendering through the already-installed Kontakt 8, and Pianoteq or the free BBC Symphony Orchestra Discover as better instruments. The next iteration should rebuild the arrangement and performance layers in code first, then run three blind A/B tests for Norbert to judge, and only then spend money.

## Each of Norbert's three verdicts traces to specific generator code

The pilot generator (`tools/xmas/arrange.py`) explains each of Norbert's verdicts. "Like a school kid performing" fits a performance layer built from independent Gaussian noise. `Style.j()` adds about **8 ms of white-noise jitter per note** at 62 BPM, and `Style.v()` adds a velocity spread with a standard deviation of 3. `arc()` adds the same sine swell to the melody in every 4-bar window. `Song.write()` emits a single tempo event, so there is no phrase-level tempo shaping and no slowing at the end; the only gesture at the close is a rolled final chord. To its credit, the piano melody already sits about 18 velocity points above the accompaniment. Its onset, however, is jittered symmetrically around the grid, and the accompaniment lands only a few ms late on average, so the deliberate 20-30 ms melody lead that skilled pianists produce is missing. The sustain pedal is lifted **0.03 beats before** each chord ends and pressed 0.04 beats after the next one starts. That gives a dry gap at every harmony change, the opposite of the syncopated pedalling described below.

"The strings sound super fake" fits a string writer whose inner voices (Vln 2, viola, cello, bass) each hold one note for the whole chord at one velocity. No CC1 or CC11 data is written. The legato violin melody starts exactly on the grid, with nothing to absorb the transition delay of a sampled legato patch. "Too slow and too simple" fits tempos of 60-68 BPM for all piano and string pilots. Fills are limited to an occasional descending chord-tone answer under held notes, and the passes are distinguished mainly by a density number and an octave shift.

| Norbert's complaint | Cause in the pilot | Fix (section) |
|---|---|---|
| "School kid performing" | White-noise jitter; one tempo; identical 4-bar swell; no melody lead; pedal gap at every change; no ritardando | Structured performance rules (next section) |
| "Strings super fake" | Static held block chords; no CC1/CC11; legato latency not offset; Simpler velocity-layered pack | String programming and orchestration (fourth section) |
| "Too slow and too simple" | 60-68 BPM fixed; hymn harmony with light colour; thin fills; little texture change between passes | Arranging craft and form (third section) |

## Pianists lead the melody by 20-30 ms and bend every phrase

**Velocity should be structured, not randomized.** In Werner Goebl's study of 22 skilled pianists on a computer-monitored Bösendorfer, "all pianists played the first voice consistently louder than the other voices". Mean melody hammer velocity was about 1.0 m/s against roughly 0.5-0.8 m/s for inner and bass voices. When pianists were asked to emphasize the melody further, it rose to 1.28 m/s while the accompaniment held its range ([Goebl 2001, JASA](https://iwk.mdw.ac.at/goebl/papers/Goebl_JASA2001_melodyLead.pdf)). The KTH rule system, the most thoroughly tested rule-based performance model, adds three shapes on top of that offset. A **phrase arch** makes each phrase "slow/soft in the beginning, fast/loud in the middle", applied at several structural levels at once. A **high-loud** rule raises level with pitch. A **melodic charge** rule emphasizes notes far from the current chord ([Friberg, Bresin & Sundberg 2006](http://www.ac-psych.org/en/download-pdf/volume/2/issue/2/id/15)). The KTH "tender" preset, which matches background carols, calls for slow tempo, low level, legato and a *small* phrase arch and small final ritard (same source). MIDI velocity curves vary by more than 60 dB across synths ([Dannenberg 2006](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rbd/papers/velocity-icmc2006.pdf)), so the numbers below must be calibrated by ear on Ableton's Grand Piano. Its velocity layers and split points were not found in any documentation. A Sound on Sound engineer scales MIDI piano velocities by 0.7-0.9 and checks where the patch switches to harder samples ([Sound on Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/better-midi-pianos)).

**Timing needs four distinct layers.** The first is **melody lead**. The melody "precedes other voices typically by around 30 ms", rising to 40-50 ms when pianists emphasize it, and the lead grows with expressiveness and skill ([Goebl 2001](https://iwk.mdw.ac.at/goebl/papers/Goebl_JASA2001_melodyLead.pdf)). On an acoustic piano this comes mostly from physics, because harder strikes reach the string sooner. Repp measured about 110 ms of onset shift between MIDI velocities 30 and 100 on a Disklavier (same source). A sample player starts every sample at its note-on, so the lead must be coded. A quadratic fitted to Repp's span, delay ≈ 0.0127·(127 − v)² ms, gives melody-at-75 against accompaniment-at-55 a lead of about 31 ms, and 70 against 60 about 16 ms. That ties lead to voicing automatically. The second layer is **chord spread**: stagger chord notes from the bottom up, and centre each cluster about one third of the way into its spread ([Sound on Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/better-midi-pianos)). The third is **phrase rubato**. In 115 commercial recordings of a Chopin étude, the dominant strategy was "major ritards at the ends of melodic gestures", and it explained 61.4% of timing variance ([Repp 1998](https://www.continuum-hypothesis.com/music/repp1.pdf)). The fourth is the **final ritardando**. Friberg and Sundberg's model is v(x) = [1 + (w^q − 1)·x]^(1/q). Listeners rated q = 2-3 highest, and measured final tempi fell between 32% and 51% of the starting tempo. The ritard starts at least 1.3 s before the last note, and the last note runs at least 1.25 times the penultimate interval ([Friberg & Sundberg 1999](https://continuum-hypothesis.com/music/Does_music_performance_allude_to_locomotion_A_mode.pdf)). With w = 0.5 and q = 2.5, tempo at successive quarters of the ritard is **100%, 91%, 81%, 68%, 50%**.

**Random noise belongs last, and it must be correlated.** KTH's noise rule made performances sound more "human" but **not more "musical"** ([KTH overview](http://www.ac-psych.org/en/download-pdf/volume/2/issue/2/id/15)). In a PLoS ONE study, 64% of listeners preferred long-range-correlated deviations to random ones, and 79% judged them more precise ([Hennig et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026457)). The current `j()` function is exactly the random kind. It should shrink to a pink-noise layer that drifts slowly and moves all notes of a chord together.

**Articulation and pedal finish the illusion.** Pianists' legato overlaps successive keys, and the overlap is longer in descending lines and in "passionate" playing. Natural staccato leaves about 60% of the interval silent, and repeated notes leave about 40% ([Bresin 2000](https://www.speech.kth.se/music/publications/thesisrb/virtual%20virtuosity%20KTHB.pdf)). For the pedal, keep CC64 at 127 and drop it to 0 briefly *just after* each new harmony, so the new chord is caught and the old one damped. Lifting early loses the connection, and lifting late blurs harmonies. Half-pedal is "overkill" for mainstream productions ([Sound on Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/better-midi-pianos)). Players read CC64 differently: Logic treats any value above 0 as down, while a Disklavier uses a threshold near 64 ([VirtuosoNet README](https://github.com/jdasam/virtuosoNet)). Send only 0 and 127 until Grand Piano's behaviour is checked.

The table below collects starting values for soft carol piano. Cited figures come from the sources above. The rest are heuristics built on them and should be tuned in a short listening session.

| Rule | Starting value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Base velocity by role | Melody 62, inner 46, bass 50, broken-chord accompaniment 42; clamp 30-90; never above ~95 | Goebl ratio; SOS 0.7-0.9 scaling |
| Phrase velocity arch | Melody +12 at the high point (or ~65% through), −6 at boundaries; accompaniment half; section level ±5; vary depth per phrase | KTH phrase arch, multi-level |
| Local accents | Downbeat +4, beat 3 of 4/4 +2; non-chord tones +5 and 5-10% longer; resolution −3; +0.4 per semitone above phrase median | KTH metrical, melodic charge, high-loud |
| Melody lead | 20 ms default, 10-35 ms scaled by velocity gap, ≤50 ms in one hand | Goebl 20-30 ms, 40-50 ms emphasized |
| Chord spread | 6-15 ms bottom-to-top on block chords; 40-70 ms per note on cadential rolls; occasional 30-50 ms bass anticipation on new sections | SOS; Goebl bass leads 35-185 ms |
| Phrase tempo | ×0.98 start, ×1.02 middle, ×0.92 over the last beat, phrase-final note +15%; scale depth by 0.5-0.7 for tender pieces | KTH tender palette; Repp ritards |
| Inner cadences | Same formula, w ≈ 0.85 over 1-2 beats | Friberg & Sundberg shape |
| Final ritard | q = 2.5, w = 0.5, ≥1.3 s; last note ≥1.25× penultimate; −8 velocity diminuendo | Friberg & Sundberg 1999 |
| Articulation | Legato melody overlap clamp(0.12×IOI, 15, 100) ms, ×1.2 descending, ×0.9 ascending; non-legato 80% of IOI; repeated notes 60%; staccato 40% | Bresin 2000; KTH 0.75 |
| Pedal | CC64 to 0 at +30 ms after the spread chord, back to 127 at +100 ms; first press after the first note | SOS syncopated pedalling |
| Noise | Onset 1/f noise, SD ~4 ms; velocity SD ~2.5 correlated over a beat; shared within chords | Hennig 2011; KTH noise |

Two implementation details matter. KTH's "melodic sync" rule computes timing on a merged voice of all onsets and transfers the result to every part, because letting the accompaniment follow a lead part "led to many practical problems" ([KTH overview](http://www.ac-psych.org/en/download-pdf/volume/2/issue/2/id/15)). Millisecond offsets also have to be converted with the *local* tempo, otherwise a slowing tempo map stretches them. The simplest way to stay DAW-agnostic is to bake all rubato into note times at a fixed project tempo rather than relying on Ableton to import a tempo map.

## Arranging craft is the bigger half of "too simple"

**Professional carol ballads start from the hymn and then layer in colour, tonicization and line.** Piano With Jonny's "Silent Night" jazz-ballad lesson starts from I/IV/V. It then adds diatonic sevenths and ninths, major and minor ii-V's into target chords, secondary dominants, altered dominants, and a 4-3 suspension such as A9sus4 → A7(b9) tonicizing Dm ([PWJ Silent Night](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/silent-night-jazz-ballad/)). The longer workshop follows a fixed production order: melody, four-part hymn, reharmonized lead sheet, left hand, fills, passing chords, intro and ending, final arrangement ([PWJ listing](https://pianowithjonny.com/music-style/holiday/page/5/)). That order maps directly onto generator stages. Learn Jazz Standards splits techniques into "inside" moves, which preserve the tune's character (diatonic thirds substitution, secondary dominants, diminished substitution), and "outside" moves (chromatic bass under a fixed melody, pedal tones, quartal stacks, borrowed chords). Its advice is to "start inside" and choose chords that contain the melody note ([LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/jazz-reharmonization-techniques/)). A GIA hymn-arranging handout adds pedal tones for intros and endings, quartal chords planed over a pedal, meter transformation (3/4 → 9/8, 4/4 → 12/8), and modulation by half step, by whole step through a sequence, or to a chromatic mediant ([Jefferson, GIA 2025](https://institute.giamusic.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Jefferson_PianoArranging_SMI25.pdf)).

**The melody check has to be automated.** A worked "Silent Night" in C shows why. Nearly every tempting tritone substitution in that tune puts the melody a minor ninth above a chord tone: G over Gb7, E over Db7, D over Db7. Only one bar (Dm9 → Db9 → C6, melody B as the b7 of Db9) is clean. A rule engine should accept melody notes that are chord tones, 6/13, 9, 11 over minor chords or #11 over maj7/dom7. It should reject a sustained natural 11 over major or dominant chords (use a sus4 chord instead), a major seventh voiced a half step under a root-melody note (use 6 or add9 instead), and any b9 clash except on a dominant resolving down a fifth. For background use, a sensible budget is mostly diatonic colour in pass one, inside moves plus secondary dominants in pass two, altered dominants at most once per four bars, no more than two chord changes per bar at 60-90 BPM, and at most one or two "outside" moments per track. That calibration is judgement; no source quantifies tasteful reharm for hotel music.

**Texture is where "school kid" lives.** PWJ's contemporary "Away in a Manger" uses add2 chords (Cadd2/E voiced D-G-C-E), rolled right-hand chords and a left hand that spans a tenth (C-G-E) and covers a wider range than the right ([PWJ Away in a Manger](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/away-in-a-manger/)). It also names the two simplest melody-harmonization devices: hold one chord under several melody notes, or run a harmony line in sixths. PianoGroove adds octave melodies with chord tones inside the octave for climaxes, and notes that a single-note melody leaves the fingers free for turns, grace notes and inner lines ([PianoGroove](https://www.pianogroove.com/jazz-piano-lessons/octaves-chord-melodies/)). Register rules come from low-interval limits: a major third is muddy below about B1-D#2 and a minor seventh below about F1-Eb2 ([Sweetwater](https://www.sweetwater.com/insync/low-interval-limit/)), so roots, fifths and octaves stay in C2-B2 and thirds and sevenths move to the tenor. Rootless left-hand voicings should keep their top note between middle C and the C above ([The Jazz Piano Site](https://www.thejazzpianosite.com/jazz-piano-lessons/jazz-chord-voicings/rootless-voicings/)). Nocturne-style accompaniment uses a low bass note with arpeggios sweeping more than two octaves under the pedal ([Wikipedia, Op. 27](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nocturnes,_Op._27_(Chopin))). New-age piano relies on open-fifth left-hand ostinatos that repeat through chord changes, and sometimes moves the ostinato to the right hand late in the piece ([FJH](https://www.fjhmusic.com/piano/display.php?item=S4133); [Sheet Music Plus, Frost](https://www.sheetmusicplus.com/en/product/frost-22733269.html)). In code this becomes a library of about eleven left-hand patterns and seven right-hand treatments, plus one hard rule: **no two consecutive passes may share both the same left-hand pattern and the same right-hand treatment.**

| Left-hand patterns (pick per pass) | Right-hand treatments (escalate per pass) |
|---|---|
| Sustained open 5th/10th; rolled 10th (60-120 ms); 1-5-10 broken chord; 2-octave nocturne arpeggio; 1-5-9-10 new-age arpeggio; stride-ballad (low root, then mid shell); guide-tone shell; alternating 2-feel bass; tonic/dominant pedal; tenor counter-line under held roots; open-5th ostinato | Single line with grace notes and turns; same-chord harmony note; parallel 6ths/3rds; rolled 2-3 note chord on beat 1 with melody on top; octaves with inner chord tones (climax only); melody in the tenor under upper chords; melody up an octave, music-box sparse |

**Fills turn held notes into conversation.** Ledutu's arranging checkpoint says to find where the melody holds or rests, fill those gaps with cadential harmony, arpeggios or scale runs, and never overpower the lead ([Ledutu](https://ledutu.com/en/learn/piano/checkpoint-create-a-piano-arrangement/)). Ted Greene recommends deriving fills and interludes from an intro figure, so the piece holds together ([Ted Greene notes](https://www.tedgreene.com/images/lessons/other/SoloGuitarStrategiesConcepts-TedGreenePersonalNotes.pdf)). "Silent Night" has a dotted half on every second bar, which gives eight or more natural slots per pass. A workable rule treats any melody note of 1.5 beats or longer, or any rest of a beat or more, as a slot. Fill it with one of the following: an echo of the last melody notes an octave down, a chord-tone descent into the next chord's third, a short pentatonic run to a step from the next melody note, a chromatic enclosure, a 4-3 or 9-8 inner-voice suspension (the cheapest "pro" gesture), an intro-motif variant, or, in later passes, a slow descant moving against the melody. Set fill velocity 15-25 below the melody, never strike a fill on a melody onset, and use at most three notes per slot in pass one and six in pass two.

**Form explains "too slow" as much as BPM does.** Sources agree on the stock moves. Borrow the last two bars for an intro, or use a turnaround or pedal-tone intro ([PWJ intros](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/3-must-know-jazz-piano-intros/)). Make the final-chorus key change only when the energy justifies it; never modulate down, and prepare the change with the new key's dominant ([Musicnotes](https://www.musicnotes.com/now/tips/a-complete-guide-to-musical-modulation/)). "Silent Night" is 24 bars of 3/4, so at 72 BPM one pass lasts 60 s. A 3:05 piece then reads as follows: a 4-bar intro on the last phrase over a dominant pedal; a sparse first pass; a 4-bar interlude from the intro motif that prepares A7; a fuller reharmonized pass a whole step up in D, with a descant and its peak in bars 17-20; a final half-pass with the melody up an octave; and a tag that repeats the last phrase over a deceptive Am9 or Bbmaj7, then Fm6, then a ritardando into a rolled 6/9 chord. For background music the energy levers should be pulled in this order: register, texture density, harmonic colour, fills, modulation, then dynamics (a total span of about 25-30 velocity steps across the track), with tempo last. On tempo itself, no source gives "correct" carol tempi. The KTH tender palette justifies slowness, but Norbert has judged the current 60-68 BPM too slow, so tempo should be tested rather than argued (see the action plan). Part of the perceived slowness is static time: no phrase-level push and long held notes with nothing happening. The rubato and fill rules above address that without changing BPM.

**A jazz trio ballad gets energy from the bass and brushes, not loudness.** A two-feel bass uses half notes on beats 1 and 3, sounds more open than walking, and leaves room for the melody ([PWJ two-feel](https://pianowithjonny.com/piano-lessons/how-to-play-a-two-feel-bass-line-on-piano/)). Ballad brushes rely on lateral sweeps, stirring in opposite directions, rather than vertical strokes ([BYU Percussion](https://percussion.byu.edu/brushes)). Rootless voicings alternate between the 3-on-bottom and 7-on-bottom shapes so that often only one note moves (Dm9 F-A-C-E to G13 F-A-B-E). Sources label these "A" and "B" inconsistently, so name them by their bottom degree in code ([The Jazz Piano Site](https://www.thejazzpianosite.com/jazz-piano-lessons/jazz-chord-voicings/rootless-voicings/); [Jazzedge](https://jazzedge.academy/?p=4706)). Build energy by moving the bass from pedal to two-feel to light quarters for 4-8 bars at the peak, then back to whole notes in the tag. Re-strike the piano's left hand only on chord changes or on the "and" before one.

## Static sustains, not the samples, make the strings sound fake

**Every long note needs a drawn dynamic.** In Spitfire's convention, CC1 crossfades between the recorded dynamic layers and changes timbre, while CC11 is a pure volume trim. Spitfire recommends using both together ([Spitfire support](https://support.spitfireaudio.com/en/articles/11816091-what-is-the-difference-between-dynamics-vibrato-and-expression)). Most libraries use velocity for short notes and CC1 for long ones ([VI-Control](https://vi-control.net/community/threads/rant-about-cc1-cc11-and-velocity.168479/)). VSL's orchestration recipes apply a CC11 "phrasing curve" to all flautando strings ([VSL Recipe 27](https://vsl.co.at/tutorials/guides/orchestration-recipes/recipe27)). One mockup critique found long notes sounding "almost sforzando" until CC11 diminuendi were drawn in ([VI-Control](https://vi-control.net/community/threads/beethovens-piano-concerto-no-2-mvmt-3-score-study-mockup.104133/)). In practice that means a slow CC1 arc per 2-4 bar phrase (floor about 35-45, peak about 70-85, up to 95-100 in the final verse) and a faster CC11 shape inside it (baseline 90-110, dips of 10-20 at phrase ends). Notes of a beat or longer get their own swell, rising 8-15 points to a peak at 30-50% of the note and tapering 10-20 by the release. Each section gets its own curve: inner voices peak half a beat to a beat later and 8-15 points lower, and basses stay flattest. Ensembles that breathe in lockstep are a classic tell. These ranges are starting values inferred from the sources, since no library publishes a canonical CC-to-dynamic map.

**Ableton's Orchestral Strings needs a workaround.** The pack is SONiVOX-recorded and loads through Simpler, "multisampled in a variety of velocities and articulations", with a Rack per instrument whose single macro switches articulations ([Ableton](https://www.ableton.com/en/packs/orchestral-strings/)). Nothing Ableton documents suggests a CC1 dynamic-layer crossfade, so velocity probably picks the layer at note-on and the sound stays put afterward. Until someone opens the Racks and confirms the mappings, the generator should map CC11 to a chain-volume or Utility gain range of about −18 to 0 dB and CC1 to Simpler's filter cutoff, so softer is also darker. An Instrument Rack's chain selector can crossfade chains, which is Live's native route to a velocity or CC crossfade between layers ([Sound on Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/creative-crossfading-ableton-live)). Live 12 **Suite** also includes the **Spitfire String Quartet** pack: close-miked solo quartet, with sul tasto, spiccato, sul ponticello and tremolo; two all-in-one presets, nine MPE presets and Max for Live devices with "delicately tuned crossfade curves" ([Ableton String Quartet](https://www.ableton.com/en/packs/string-quartet/)). For intimate first verses it suits carols better than the ensemble, and layering its sul tasto under ensemble sustains adds detail. The **free BBC Symphony Orchestra Discover** (Spitfire plugin, any DAW) responds to CC1 dynamics in the conventional way ([Sound on Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/news/spitfires-bbcso-discover-now-instantly-free)). It is the cheapest test of whether the library or the programming is the bottleneck.

**Legato lines must be monophonic, overlapped and pulled early.** True-legato patches play a recorded transition when notes overlap, so a melody track must never contain chords. Cinematic Studio Strings documents how much delay this costs: velocity 0-64 gives a slow transition of about 333 ms, 65-100 a medium of about 250 ms, and 101-127 a fast of about 100 ms. The developers say the delay "cannot be changed" and advise playing ahead of the beat ([Sound on Sound CSS review](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/cinematic-studio-strings); [CSS](https://cinematicstudioseries.com/strings/)). BBC SO legatos work at −120 to −150 ms of track delay, and the first note of each phrase speaks earlier than the transitions and needs nudging back ([VI-Control](https://vi-control.net/community/threads/string-melodies-sounding-displaced-in-higher-tempi.142171/)). At 60-70 BPM a beat lasts 860-1000 ms, so a 100-250 ms late melody is very audible, and it is a likely contributor to "fake". The generator should shift note-ons per articulation instead of relying on Live's per-track delay: legato and sustain longs −60 to −150 ms (start at −100 and measure), shorts −20 to −60 ms, pizzicato 0 to −20 ms, harp and celesta 0 to −10 ms. Overlap legato notes by 20-60 ms and leave 50-150 ms gaps at phrase breaks for a fresh attack. Measure the real latency of "Violin Section Legato" by rendering one note on a grid line, because whether it has sampled transitions is undocumented.

**Orchestration fixes what CC cannot.** Space chords wide at the bottom and closer on top, as the harmonic series does ([Sweetwater](https://www.sweetwater.com/insync/make-orchestrations-sound-more-balanced/)). Replace held block chords with voice-led lines: inner parts step to the nearest chord tone, add 4-3 and 9-8 suspensions, re-articulate long chords in half notes or dotted-quarter-plus-eighth rhythms, and stagger inner entries on beat two. Double cellos and basses in octaves on the bass line, and double violins in octaves only for the climactic verse. Plan articulation per verse: solo or quartet violin over sul tasto violas and cellos with harp in verse one, pizzicato low strings on beats 1 and 3 in verse two, full arco with octave doublings in the last. Harpists use at most four notes per hand within an octave plus a fourth and roll block chords upward. Low fast arpeggios "sound muddy and inarticulate" ([composingforharp.com](https://composingforharp.com/?p=7)), so keep harp arpeggios mostly above C3 and use one glissando per section at most. Celesta and glockenspiel should double the melody an octave or two up only on chosen phrases, 6-12 dB under it.

**One shared hall glues the mockup.** A shared film-score template uses hall reverb with **14 ms pre-delay and a 2.5 s tail**, and a church preset at **18 ms and 3.2 s** ([VI-Control](https://vi-control.net/community/threads/how-do-you-get-that-orchestral-film-score-sound.101659/)). Use a short, early-reflection-heavy reverb for stage placement plus a long tail, and balance only after the reverb is set ([Steinberg forum](https://forums.steinberg.net/t/help-with-realistic-mock-ups/157194)). Give bass instruments little reverb and high-pass the returns ([Cinematic Composing](https://learn.cinematiccomposing.com/blog/composing-part-iv-eq-reverb)). Pan by seating: violins left, celli right, basses centre-right ([Loopmasters](https://www.loopmasters.com/articles/2361-Realistic-Orchestral-Panning-Instrument-Placement-In-The-Stereo-Field)). For EQ, high-pass the string bus around 60 Hz and violins around 180-200 Hz, make gentle cuts of about −3 dB in the 200-350 Hz mud zone, and add at most +1-2 dB around 2 kHz ([Music Guy Mixing](https://www.musicguymixing.com/eq-strings/)). In Ableton terms: one Hybrid Reverb send at 2.5-3.2 s decay, 12-20 ms pre-delay, return high-passed at 200-300 Hz; basses 10-15 dB less send than violins; harp and celesta slightly wetter.

## Lo-fi needs one shared groove, not swung hats over a straight band

**The current beat is mildly swung in one place and dead straight everywhere else.** Lo-fi and chillhop sit at 60-90 BPM, usually 70-90 ([Yamaha Hub](https://hub.yamaha.com/proaudio/recording/how-to-create-lo-fi); [Native Instruments](https://blog.native-instruments.com/lo-fi-hip-hop/)), so the pilot's 80-84 BPM is in genre. MPC swing runs from 50% (straight) to 75%: about 54% for modern hip-hop, 58% for classic boom-bap, and 62% for neo-soul shuffle ([Audeobox](https://www.audeobox.com/learn/mpc-software/mpc-drum-programming/)). Attack Magazine notes swing can go on bass, keys and leads too, at different amounts per element ([Attack](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/tutorials/going-off-grid-what-is-swing-and-how-to-add-it/)). For 16th swing, the offbeat delay is (S/100 − 0.5) × (30000/BPM) ms: at 80 BPM, 56% gives 22.5 ms, 58% gives 30 ms and 62% gives 45 ms. The pilot's `drum_bar` delays offbeat 8ths by 0.04 beats, about 30 ms at 80 BPM, which is only about 54% 8th-note swing, and applies it to the drums alone. When keys, bass and melody stay straight, the parts disagree on the grid, and adding random jitter to everything makes it worse. "Dilla time" is the deliberate version of disagreement. Charnas describes hats implying straight time while kicks lean late and snares land early, with one signature snare displacement of about 65 ms ([The Pier](https://thepier.org/twenty-years-of-dilla-time-how-j-dilla-rewrote-the-feel-of-popular-music/); [LRB](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v44/n20/francis-gooding/basement-beats)). The fix is one shared 16th swing of about 56-58% across drums, bass, keys and melody, with small *systematic* per-voice offsets (kick 5-10 ms late, snare −5 to +10 ms) and random jitter of ±3-5 ms or none.

**Drums stay sparse, ghosted and dark.** Two or three elements are enough: snare or rim on 2 and 4, a kick on 1 plus one or two syncopations, and hat or shaker ([Mode Audio](https://modeaudio.com/magazine/lofi-hip-hop-5-production-essentials)). Ghost notes sit at about 30-40% velocity on the 16th before 2 and 4, and "if you can clearly identify them as separate hits, they are too loud" ([Soundbridge](https://soundbridge.io/en/drum-programming-tips-for-producers-of-all-levels)). Low-pass each kit element to about 2 kHz ([Mode Audio](https://modeaudio.com/magazine/lofi-hip-hop-5-production-essentials)) or from 3.12 kHz ([Native Instruments](https://blog.native-instruments.com/lo-fi-hip-hop/)). Drums often enter only after several bars of chords alone ([Yamaha Hub](https://hub.yamaha.com/proaudio/recording/how-to-create-lo-fi)). In MIDI terms: kick 105-115 on downbeats and 85-95 syncopated; snare backbeat 100-110 and ghosts 30-45; hats 80-95 on beats and 55-70 off. Move a second kick every fourth or eighth bar, and carry a fill or drop-out in bar 8 of each section.

**Treat the carol as a sample, not a lead sheet.** Lo-fi harmony is 2-4 chord loops of sevenths and ninths, such as I-vi-ii-V or ii-V-I, each held one or two bars ([Flat.io](https://blog.flat.io/lofi-chord-progressions/)). Leave melody timing unquantized and answer it with a counter-melody ([Native Instruments](https://blog.native-instruments.com/lo-fi-hip-hop/)). The genre's flip technique chops and rearranges a known source into a new phrase ([Melodics](https://melodics.com/blog/what-is-a-sample-flip)). For "Jingle Bells" that means a 1-2 bar hook motif displaced to start on the "and", with notes dropped and the last note lengthened. Glockenspiel doubles it 12 dB down on repeats only, a chord stab answers it, and the full melody appears once, mid-track. Keep keys voiced in C3-C5 with the root an octave below, strum chords 10-30 ms bottom-up, and set the top note 10 velocity points above the inner voices. Bass plays roots in a simple rhythm, low-passed "right back to 150Hz or less", with keys sidechained to the kick ([Mode Audio](https://modeaudio.com/magazine/lofi-hip-hop-5-production-essentials)). For background music, about 2-4 dB of ducking is plenty (a heuristic). Form is a loop that changes one layer at every 8-bar boundary: intro, A, B, a breakdown without drums, A, outro, with slow low-pass automation over 8-16 bars ([Violet Recording](https://violetrecording.com/how-to-make-lofi-music/)). At 80 BPM, 60 bars run 3:00.

**Degradation should be felt, not heard, especially in a hotel.** A typical Ableton chain is Saturator → Redux or Vinyl Distortion → Auto Filter low-pass → Chorus-Ensemble in Vibrato mode → Reverb ([Mode Audio](https://modeaudio.com/magazine/how-to-produce-a-lofi-sound-in-any-daw); [Splice](https://splice.com/blog/lo-fi-piano-ableton/)). Splice suggests Frequency Shifter wobble around 5 Hz LFO amount at 2-3 Hz rate, and crackle "but don't overdo it" (same Splice source). Every texture layer should sit "just below conscious hearing" ([Violet Recording](https://violetrecording.com/how-to-make-lofi-beats/)). Starting points to tune by ear: keys Saturator drive +3 to +6 dB at 30-50% wet, a mild 10-12 bit Redux, a 12 dB/oct low-pass at 3-5 kHz, and a 1.5-2.5 s reverb send with a high cut near 5 kHz. Drums go through a light Drum Buss with low-pass at 2.5-3.5 kHz. Crackle sits far down in the mix, lighter than streaming lo-fi, because venues play it quietly for hours and noise can read as bad audio.

**The master should be consistent rather than loud.** Spotify normalizes to **−14 LUFS** and asks for true peak below −1 dBTP ([Spotify for Artists](https://support.spotify.com/artists/article/loudness-normalization/)). Apple Music reportedly uses −16 LUFS ([Production Expert](https://www.production-expert.com/production-expert-1/apple-choose-16lufs-loudness-level-for-apple-music-heres-why)). A Glue Compressor at 2:1 with 1-2 dB of gain reduction on the drum bus is the light-touch norm ([Music Guy Mixing](https://www.musicguymixing.com/drum-bus-compression/)). For Beat Breeze and in-venue use, a −16 to −14 LUFS master with a −1 dBTP ceiling and intact dynamics, held consistent across the catalog, fits better than a streaming-loud master. That is an extrapolation; no source gives venue-specific targets.

**Live 12's own tools are useful but interactive.** The Groove Pool's Base, Quantize, Timing, Random and Velocity controls apply MPC and SP-1200 swing templates, and Commit writes the groove into the notes ([Ableton manual, grooves](https://www.ableton.com/en/manual/using-grooves/)). Live 12 adds per-note **Chance** and **Velocity Deviation**, which vary every loop pass, plus Strum, Ornament, Span and Velocity Shaper transformations that rewrite notes destructively ([Ableton manual, MIDI tools](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/midi-tools/); [Editing MIDI](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/editing-midi/)). A generator should bake strum, flams, swing and velocity curves into the notes. It can use the playback-time fields, Chance at 60-80% on ghost hats and extra kicks and Velocity Deviation of ±8-12 on hats, through the `.als` XML. The note attribute names there (`Probability`, `VelocityDeviation` on `MidiNoteEvent`) should be confirmed by saving a Live 12 set and inspecting it. Live's own Humanize can move notes by up to a quarter of a grid step, about 47 ms at 1/16 and 80 BPM, which is far too much at high settings.

## Tools raise the floor, but none of them supplies the taste

**For piano performance, one model is worth an A/B against hand-coded rules.** **Pianist Transformer** (December 2025, Apache-2.0) takes a score MIDI and outputs expressive MIDI with a DAW-importable tempo map and per-note pedal, and it runs on CPU ([arXiv 2512.02652](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02652); [GitHub](https://github.com/yhj137/PianistTransformer)). **VirtuosoNet** took first place at RenCon 2025 but needs MusicXML input and composer conditioning, and has **no licence file**, so by default it is all rights reserved until the author grants permission ([GitHub](https://github.com/jdasam/virtuosoNet)). **ScorePerformer** is CC BY-NC-SA and so non-commercial ([GitHub](https://github.com/ilya16/ScorePerformer)). **midihum** rewrites only velocities ([GitHub](https://github.com/erwald/midihum/blob/master/README.md)). **Aria** (EleutherAI, Apache-2.0, MLX backend for Apple Silicon) continues piano MIDI from a prompt rather than performing a given score, and warns it "may memorize or closely reproduce" famous works ([GitHub](https://github.com/EleutherAI/aria)). All of these were trained on classical competition repertoire, so expect them to over-rubato simple homophonic carols. A rule set tuned to the KTH tender palette is more controllable. The best pattern is a hybrid: code voicing, lead and pedal from the structure the generator already knows, and borrow a model's tempo or velocity curve as one optional layer.

**For composition, models are candidate generators that Claude curates.** The **Anticipatory Music Transformer** (Apache-2.0 code and weights) treats a melody as controls and writes accompaniment. Human evaluators rated it close to human-composed music over 20-second clips ([arXiv 2306.08620](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08620); [GitHub](https://github.com/jthickstun/anticipation)). **MIDI-GPT** (MIT) regenerates chosen bars or tracks with density and polyphony controls, though its models are still marked "training in progress" ([GitHub](https://github.com/Metacreation-Lab/MIDI-GPT)). **Composer's Assistant 2** (MIT) found no significant listening difference from real music in co-creative use, and was trained only on permissively licensed MIDI ([arXiv 2407.14700](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14700); [arXiv 2301.12525](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12525)). That makes it the lowest provenance risk, but it runs inside REAPER. Magenta v1, Microsoft Muzic/MuseCoco, note-seq and sfizz are archived and should not be foundations ([magenta](https://github.com/magenta/magenta); [muzic](https://github.com/microsoft/muzic); [sfizz](https://github.com/sfztools/sfizz)). Code licences permit commercial use, but training-data provenance differs: Aria learned from transcribed recordings, and Anticipation from the Lakh MIDI corpus. BMAsia should get a legal view before shipping catalog tracks whose notes a model wrote.

**Ableton control is solved; Ableton export is not.** **ahujasid/ableton-mcp** (MIT, 3,165 stars, pushed 2026-09-22) creates tracks and clips, writes notes and loads browser devices. Its anonymous telemetry is on by default (disable with `ABLETON_MCP_DISABLE_TELEMETRY=true`), and it has an opt-in dataset mode whose recordings "may be published" ([GitHub](https://github.com/ahujasid/ableton-mcp)). A 2026 guide says it has "no render-to-WAV tool", that third-party presets are "hit or miss", and that it works best as a scaffolder ([mcp.directory](https://mcp.directory/blog/ableton-mcp-complete-guide-2026)). **jpoindexter/ableton-mcp** claims 200+ tools, including freeze/flatten, clip automation envelopes and rack macros ([GitHub](https://github.com/jpoindexter/ableton-mcp)). That matters for writing CC curves into Rack macros and for a "render via freeze files" experiment. The one MCP that advertises export reportedly returns "501 unsupported" ([Glama](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/jterrats/ableton-live-mcp/tools/ableton_export_render)). The new Ableton Extensions SDK (JS/TS, Suite, 12.4.5+ beta) does not mention export ([Ableton](https://www.ableton.com/live/extensions)). Export stays a GUI step. **glincker/ableton-skills** (MIT, 12 skills covering voice leading, velocity curves and mix diagnostics) is a free knowledge layer worth reviewing before installing, given its 35 stars ([GitHub](https://github.com/glincker/ableton-skills)).

**Headless rendering is the biggest automation win.** **Spotify Pedalboard** (GPL-3.0, actively maintained) loads Audio Unit and VST3 *instruments* on macOS and renders timestamped MIDI to audio in Python ([API reference](https://spotify.github.io/pedalboard/reference/pedalboard.html)). The installed Kontakt 8 and Surge XT could therefore render stems with no Save dialog. Whether Kontakt behaves without a GUI host, and how to restore a chosen patch from saved state, still needs a smoke test. **DawDreamer** adds graph routing and PPQN automation ([GitHub](https://github.com/DBraun/DawDreamer)). **Pianoteq** has a documented `--headless --midi --wav` command line and is modelled rather than sampled, so it responds continuously to velocity and pedal ([Modartt forum](https://forum.modartt.com/viewtopic.php?id=11200)). It is the likeliest single upgrade for the piano playlists; price and commercial terms were not checked. **REAPER** is the stronger automation host, with an MCP that includes `render_project` and `render_stems` ([bonfire-systems/reaper-mcp](https://github.com/bonfire-systems/reaper-mcp)), but it cannot load Ableton's internal instruments. The trade-off runs throughout: headless paths lose Grand Piano, Orchestral Strings and the Suite packs, which are the current sound. For the core MIDI code, **pretty_midi** (MIT) or **symusic** (MIT) handle tempo maps, CC and pedal reliably, and **music21** (BSD-3) adds harmony analysis and MusicXML export ([pretty_midi](https://github.com/craffel/pretty-midi); [symusic](https://github.com/Yikai-Liao/symusic); [music21](https://github.com/cuthbertLab/music21)). They are cheap replacements for the hand-rolled writer in `Song.write()`.

**Licensing has one live risk.** Spitfire permits commercial recordings made with its libraries, including the free tiers, and bans using the sounds to build another sample library. Its FAQ is silent on AI ([Spitfire support](https://support.spitfireaudio.com/en/articles/11815239-are-spitfire-audio-sample-libraries-royalty-free-and-can-i-use-them-on-commercial-recordings)). Audiobro's EULA explicitly prohibits generative-AI training on its samples ([Audiobro](https://audiobro.com/eula/)), and Toontrack and Loopmasters carry similar clauses ([VI-Control](https://vi-control.net/community/threads/toontracks-new-eula-forbids-ai-training.154821/); [Loopmasters](https://help.loopmasters.com/hc/en-us/articles/7718328554772)). Rendering finished tracks is the permitted use. Uploading those renders to Mureka as references, or using them as training data, is where an AI clause would bite. Read each vendor's full EULA, Ableton's pack licence included, before doing that.

The order follows expected audible gain per hour of work. Arrangement and performance code fixes come first because they cost nothing and address all three complaints. Experiments follow, each producing blind A/B renders that go to Norbert in full, in line with the standing rule that he judges every sample. Purchases come last, decided by those results.

## Conclusion

Most of the gap between the pilot and a professional arrangement is information the generator already has and does not use. It knows which note is the melody, where every phrase ends, which bars hold a long note and when the harmony changes. Those are the inputs every cited performance rule, fill rule and pedal rule needs. The pilot spends that knowledge on random numbers instead. That makes iteration two mostly a code-structure job, and its result is testable: render the velocity and inter-onset curves. If they show arches, phrase-final bumps and a melody always on top, the performance layer is working before anyone listens.

The other shift is in where money should go. Better samples and AI models are tempting answers to "sounds fake", but the evidence suggests they pay off only after the programming is right. A static, block-chord string part sounds fake through any library, and a learned performance model trained on Chopin will not fix a hymn-harmony arrangement with no fills. Fixing programming first, running the three blind tests, and only then buying Pianoteq or a string library means every purchase is justified by Norbert's own ears rather than by a product page. The same order also keeps the catalog clean of provenance questions.
